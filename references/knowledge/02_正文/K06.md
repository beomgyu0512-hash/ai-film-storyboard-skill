## ABSTRACT

Vertical micro-dramas – ultra-short, serialised narratives designed for mobile-first platforms – have become increasingly visible internationally across the 2024–2026 period, while remaining rooted in earlier Chinese micro-drama, web fiction and platform-native production cultures. This paper offers a critically informed review of recent creative shifts in the form, examining how mobile-specific conditions – vertical framing, compressed duration, cliff-hanger-led rhythm, rapid genre recycling, audience feedback and AI-assisted production – are reshaping storytelling practice. Rather than treating micro-drama as either a passing social-media novelty or a settled industry form, the article maps its movement into a more international, AI-assisted, genre-cyclical and industrially contested screen practice. It considers chat-based storytelling, interactive and branching possibilities, vertical cinematic language, AI production pipelines, genre experimentation, adaptation and feedback-responsive authorship. The article argues that vertical micro-drama is becoming a significant mobile-native storytelling form, while also remaining shaped by formulaic repetition, platform analytics, production speed and data-driven risk management.

## The rise of vertical micro-drama: why this format is more than a fad

Mobile-first vertical micro-dramas – serialised video stories designed for mobile-first platforms – have consolidated as a distinctive screen form across the 2024–2026 period. Unlike typical viral clips, micro-dramas are structured as very short serial episodes, often running around 60–90 s, although episode length varies by platform and opening episodes may be longer, sometimes approaching three minutes. Their defining feature is not simply brevity, but the use of compressed serial form to sustain narrative tension, build cliff-hangers and encourage cumulative engagement across episodes (Scanlon 2025). Each instalment functions like a compressed television episode, complete with characters, plot arcs and hooks designed to compel continued viewing.

This article does not treat 2024–2026 as the origin point of vertical micro-drama. Rather, it understands this period as one of intensified international visibility, market consolidation, AI acceleration and wider platform-industrial debate. The form has strong antecedents in Chinese micro-drama, short-form video culture, web fiction, platform-native production and data-led commissioning. Chinese-backed and English-language platforms have expanded the international circulation of these practices, while recurring genre formulas, rapid localisation and mobile-first distribution have made the format increasingly visible across Asia, Europe and North America. Gist's practitioner account of Chinese-financed vertical series produced in Australia shows that this internationalisation also depends upon cross-cultural development structures in which Chinese app teams, bilingual producers and English-language writers collaborate to adapt stories for Anglophone audiences (Gist 2025, 190–193).

A 2025 overseas short-drama industry report by 短剧自习室, based on 334 overseas short-drama apps from January to December 2025, reports total annual downloads of 1.199 billion and total revenue of US$2.329 billion, representing year-on-year growth of 268% and 133% respectively; the report also characterises 2025 as a year of rapid growth, market restructuring, globalisation and increased involvement from traditional film, television and technology companies (短剧自习室 2025). In China itself, 智研咨询 (2026) reports that the domestic micro-drama market grew from 37.39 billion RMB in 2023 to 50.44 billion RMB in 2024, exceeding mainland China's cinema box office of 42.502 billion RMB in the same year. Dedicated apps are therefore generating substantial revenues and large-scale user engagement across Asia and the West (Arkenberg et al. 2025). This commercial growth has attracted increasing attention from established studios, technology companies and investors, positioning vertical micro-drama as an increasingly recognised part of the contemporary screen industries.

The article is therefore framed as a critically informed review of recent developments rather than a comprehensive critical evaluation of the industry as a whole. Its contribution is to offer a periodised mapping of how vertical micro-drama is moving from platform-native serial melodrama into a more international, AI-assisted, genre-cyclical and industrially contested screen form. The focus is not on platform economics alone, but on how creative practice is being reshaped by mobile-specific conditions: vertical framing, compressed duration, cliff-hanger-led rhythm, rapid genre recycling, audience feedback loops, AI-assisted production and the need to localise stories across markets.

Given the rapid development of vertical micro-drama and the limited availability of peer-reviewed scholarship on the most recent 2024–2026 developments, the article necessarily draws on industry journalism, platform reporting and trade commentary as primary sources for emerging practice, while treating these sources as situated evidence rather than neutral accounts. Where the same trade or journalistic sources recur, this reflects the current availability of contemporaneous reporting on a rapidly developing field; to address the risk of over-reliance, the article triangulates these accounts where possible through industry reports, platform examples, practitioner commentary and emerging academic research. Recent academic work has begun to examine these production processes more closely. Cao et al. (2026), drawing on interviews with

28 micro-drama writers, creators and practitioners, frame micro-drama authorship as a feedback-responsive process shaped by the interaction of writers, audiences and platforms. Their study supports the argument that platform analytics, audience comments, reposts, memes and other engagement signals do not simply measure audience response after publication, but can actively shape topic selection, character emphasis, narrative pacing and production workflow (Cao et al. 2026).

The sections that follow examine creative developments visible across the 2024–2026 period, including interactive and chat-based storytelling, vertical cinematic language, AI-assisted production, genre cycling, adaptation, audience engagement and feedback-responsive authorship. Together, these developments suggest that vertical micro-drama should be understood neither as a passing social-media novelty nor as a fully stabilised screen form, but as an evolving mobile-native narrative practice shaped by the interaction of creative experiment, industrial scale, platform analytics and audience behaviour.

## Interactive & branching narratives

One frontier being explored is interactive micro-drama, where viewers are invited to influence the direction of the story. Interactive films such as Bandersnatch (Slade 2018) demonstrated the appeal of choice-based storytelling, but also showed how mid-story decision-making can overwhelm viewers and fracture narrative coherence. In 2025, Lauk-Dubitskiy proposed a model tailored to micro-dramas: allowing audiences to configure certain plot parameters before watching, rather than requiring real-time branching during the story (Lauk-Dubitskiy 2025). This concept, dubbed CLOKI, would allow viewers to select a preferred storyline path or episode duration in advance, delivering a personalised short-form drama without disrupting coherence (Lauk-Dubitskiy 2025). While still theoretical, it suggests one way in which micro-dramas might incorporate choice-based structures in a more user-friendly form.

In practice, however, the more visible development is not fully branching narrative, but feedback-responsive story design. Some apps in China are already experimenting with multi-threaded storylines and A/B testing plots, using AI to iterate different story outcomes quickly and identify which versions engage viewers (Ramachandran 2025). Cao et al. (2026) similarly identify 'audience-responsive micro-dramas' as a distinctive production mode, in which writers adapt storylines in response to real-time audience feedback, including comments, reposts and memes. These developments suggest that interactivity in micro-drama is developing along at least three related paths: explicit choice-based design, where viewers make decisions before or during viewing; data-led iteration, where audience behaviour indirectly shapes subsequent production; and post-episode character interaction. Character.AI's July 2026 launch of c.ai Series extended the authored episode into a conversational space by allowing viewers to chat or role-play with characters from Last Summer, The Nighttime Game and Eden Fall after viewing, while constraining each episode's language model to information already established onscreen (Pulliam-Moore 2026). Practical adoption of explicit branching remains uneven, but feedback-responsive production is already becoming central to platform-native micro-drama,

while conversational character systems point towards a further extension of the form beyond the episode itself (Cao et al. 2026; Lauk-Dubitskiy 2025; Ramachandran 2025).

## Chat-based storytelling

A notable storytelling innovation gaining traction is the use of messaging and social media interfaces as the narrative medium. In 2025, Sydney Jo Robinson's TikTok miniseries The Group Chat went viral by presenting its drama almost entirely through a simulated text message thread (Gawley 2025). The series depicts the conflicts of a fictional girlfriend's group chat, including one friend inviting her boyfriend to a girls-only dinner and sparking comic tension within the group (Gawley 2025). The visual format mirrors a smartphone chat screen, with messages and audio notes conveying each character's voice. The approach attracted significant attention: the first episode amassed around 30 million views and drew celebrity attention, including Charlie Puth voicing one of the characters in a later instalment (Gawley 2025; People 2025). Subsequent coverage suggests that The Group Chat developed beyond an isolated viral post into a continuing TikTok-native serial, with Robinson completing two seasons and planning further instalments (Glamour 2025).

The significance of The Group Chat is that it shows how screen-life storytelling, familiar from desktop thrillers, texting-fiction apps and social media performance, can be adapted to micro-drama without requiring conventional location shooting or complex production infrastructure. The series turns the phone interface itself into the dramatic space, using typing, voice notes, pauses, group dynamics and comment-driven speculation as part of the audience experience. This also makes the form highly compatible with TikTok's creator culture, where viewers respond in real time, role-play in comments and debate characters' decisions between episodes. Chat-based micro-drama therefore offers a low-cost but highly legible model of vertical storytelling: one in which the platform interface is not simply a distribution channel but becomes part of the narrative form.

## Vertical cinematic language

A crucial adaptation in micro-drama is the development and refinement of a visual grammar for the vertical 9:16 screen. This includes the practical challenge of staging multiple characters within a narrow frame, where two-shots and three-shots require careful eyeline management and often lead time-pressured productions towards tightly framed single-character compositions. Practitioners emphasise that effective vertical storytelling is not simply a matter of cropping horizontal video; it requires its own 'cinematic language' (Scanlon 2025). Experienced vertical editors such as Ren Chih Chen focus on the format's natural intimacy, using close-ups and centred compositions to give emotional weight to stories viewed in the palm of the hand (Stojan 2025). 'You lose the natural width for landscapes and group shots, so every choice has to be more deliberate,' Chen notes, yet this constraint can become an advantage: the vertical frame feels inherently intimate, with facial expressions and details amplified on a personal screen (Stojan 2025).

To hold mobile attention, micro-drama editing also depends on accelerated rhythm and contrast. Stojan describes the use of slow, lingering close-ups for vulnerable emotional beats, alongside rapid cuts and tight framing for confrontation or action.

(Stojan 2025). This rhythmic ebb and flow ensures that the story ‘never stalls,’ which is critical when viewers are one swipe away from distraction (Stojan 2025). Another tactic is to establish familiar genre cues immediately, such as quick flashes of a cowboy on horseback in a western romance, while trimming any filler that does not drive the core story (Stojan 2025).

Vertical micro-drama also depends on a wider audiovisual grammar than framing alone. Many series use onscreen text, graphic overlays, visual effects, sound effects, internal voice-over and rapid shifts in music to make story information immediately legible. These devices are not simply decorative. They compress exposition, signal character status, clarify relationships, punctuate emotional reversals and mark tonal shifts within very short episodes. Internal voice-over can externalise suspicion, desire or withheld knowledge without requiring longer dialogue scenes, while sound effects and abrupt music cues intensify revelations, confrontations and cliff-hangers. In this sense, vertical cinematic language is not only a matter of close-ups and centred composition, but of layered audiovisual signalling designed to accelerate narrative comprehension and emotional response.

Together, these techniques show how micro-drama creators work within extreme brevity: conveying character and conflict within seconds through vertical-friendly angles, layered sound and image cues, and suspenseful cliff-hangers. This precision differentiates micro-dramas from casual short-form clips. As one analyst observes, these bite-size serials rely on 'outsized emotions [and] sharp plot twists' optimised for shareability on social platforms (Scanlon 2025). As the format has professionalised, many micro-dramas have developed tighter scripts and more polished directing despite their length (Scanlon 2025).

## AI-powered production and creative tools

One significant development in the 2024–2026 period is the increasing visibility of AI-assisted production in micro-drama creation, from scripting through post-production. Industry reporting by Fischer and Flynn describes some leading vertical-drama studios as 'AI-first' content companies, although the paywalled nature of this source means the claim is treated here as reported industry positioning rather than independently verified evidence (Fischer and Flynn 2026). For example, Ukraine-based startup HolyWater, which runs the micro-drama app MyDrama, uses generative AI in multiple ways: to create vertical video content via its 'MyMuse' engine, automate dubbing and localisation, and prototype story concepts. 'We create AI pilots to get data very quickly,' HolyWater CEO Anatolii Kasianov explains, allowing the team to test and refine ideas before committing to full production (Fischer and Flynn 2026). This rapid iteration cycle allows creators to experiment with plotlines and genres at lower cost, then concentrate resources on the narratives that appear to resonate.

Perhaps the most dramatic example came in Q3 2025, when South Korea's platform Vigloo released two fully AI-produced micro-dramas (Daswani 2025c). These were 30-minute vertical series: a romance titled Met a Savior in Hell and a sci-fi thriller titled Seoul: 2053, both created end-to-end with artificial intelligence. Reporting on Vigloo's production claims, Daswani states that Met a Savior in Hell was completed in just six weeks, cutting production costs by 90% and halving the usual timeline (Daswani 2025c). In

practice, human creators oversaw the process, but AI handled labour-intensive tasks such as generating backgrounds and special effects, autotuning the editing pace, and voicing characters in multiple languages. 'Human creativity remains central, with AI amplifying what's possible,' notes Vigloo CEO Neil Choi, stressing that the aim is to use AI to elevate storytelling rather than replace writers or directors (Daswani 2025c).

AI is also becoming embedded across the wider micro-drama value chain. In China, where micro-drama production is most developed, AI tools are already used for personalised content discovery, faster script iteration, branching story tests and AI-driven character rendering (Ramachandran 2025). This industrial scale is significant: some large studios reportedly produce more than 1,000 vertical dramas annually, creating production conditions in which rapid iteration, formula testing and data-led commissioning become central to the form. Industry commentary by Mao (2026) frames AI micro-drama as a new phase of the category, defined by speed, scale and synthetic creativity, in which micro-drama becomes not only a fast content format but a rapid production system. This scale also helps explain why AI-assisted production has become strategically important: in a market that has already surpassed domestic cinema box office in China, production speed, cost control, localisation and workflow automation become industrial pressures rather than optional technical experiments (智研咨询 2026).

Recent technical research suggests that AI short-drama generation is beginning to move beyond one-shot script expansion towards systems designed around the specific formal demands of the genre, including opening hooks, conflict escalation, ending suspense, spatial continuity and production-level quality control (Shi et al. 2026). Some Chinese micro-drama studios were already controversially using AI to replace actors and screenwriters for low-budget productions in 2025 (Teng 2025). In a Guangming Daily report carried by Xinhua, Wang (2026) reports that full-process AI production can reduce the cost of a single short-drama episode from 50,000–100,000 RMB to under 5,000 RMB, while reducing production cycles from around one month to several days. The same report states that national short-drama filming starts fell by around 75% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026, with some small and medium production teams shifting in batches towards AI comic dramas and AI synthetic-human short dramas (Wang 2026).

These developments suggest that AI is not simply adding isolated tools to existing workflows but accelerating the standardisation and industrialisation of micro-drama production. Wang (2026) argues that AI systems extract core elements from recurring genres such as rebirth, wealthy-family melodrama and domineering-CEO narratives, break plots into standardised 'emotional components,' and align production rhythm with user behaviour data. As an industry source rather than an academic study, Mao (2026) should also be treated cautiously, but the article is useful in identifying sector concerns around saturation: it reports that more than 10,000 AI-generated micro-drama titles were being released monthly in China by early 2026, with AI-generated content in top charts rising from 7% to nearly 40% within a year. The result, according to one report, has been algorithm-generated shorts that 'rack up tens of millions of views with no actors, no crews, and barely any human input' (WION 2025). While this raises ethical questions, and has prompted industry guilds to seek new protections, it also demonstrates the disruptive potential of AI in short-form production. At the same time, Wang (2026) notes that AI still struggles to replace the 'living' quality of human performance and the subtle grasp

of human feeling, suggesting that AI’s impact should be understood as both industrially disruptive and aesthetically limited. Fruit Love Island offered a different indication of AI-native form rather than simple production substitution. Released in March 2026 as 22 two-to-four-minute episodes, the series reworked reality-dating conventions through anthropomorphised fruit characters, coupling ceremonies and cliff-hanger eliminations (C21Media 2026). Its significance lies less in visual realism than in demonstrating how synthetic production can support improbable casts, rapid serialisation and a deliberately absurd platform-native aesthetic.

Shi et al. (2026) propose in One Sentence, One Drama, a hierarchical multi-agent framework that transforms a single-sentence idea into a complete short drama through structured story generation, 3D-grounded first-frame generation, multi-stage reviewer loops, scene transition planning and background music (BGM) matching. The significance of this work for the present discussion is not that it demonstrates established industry practice, but that it identifies the technical problems AI systems must solve if they are to produce short dramas that meet platform-native narrative expectations: dense dramatic hooks, rapid escalation, coherent reversals, cross-clip spatial consistency and audio-visual continuity (Shi et al. 2026).

AI is also enabling micro-dramas to scale globally. Automated translation and dubbing tools are now commonly used to distribute series across languages. For instance, the romance series The Secret Life of Amy Bensen, originally an English-language show, was reformatted into a 35-episode vertical drama and dubbed in eight languages to reach new audiences (Daswani 2025b). Emerging AI voice-cloning services make it feasible for even small studios to release multilingual versions of their micro-dramas almost simultaneously, while AI-driven app infrastructures analyse drop-off rates and viewer comments in real time, helping creators adjust storylines or clip lengths to maximise engagement in subsequent episodes. Fischer and Flynn report that HolyWater's revenue grew threefold in 2025 using this data-driven production model (Fischer and Flynn 2026).

Finally, AI-assisted production is lowering barriers for independent creators to enter the field. Text-to-video generators and mobile editing apps with AI features allow a single creator to add effects or generate virtual sets on a small budget. As an example, one tool, Genra.ai, advertises the ability to 'replace physical production – no actors, no locations, no cameras – executing a shoot that once cost $300,000 for a tiny fraction of that' (Fischer and Flynn 2026). By late 2025, some Hollywood companies were also reported to be investing in these techniques for micro-content. Fischer and Flynn report that Fox Entertainment took an equity stake in HolyWater, citing its 'disciplined, data-driven model' of AI-augmented production as a key asset (Fischer and Flynn 2026). Industry commentary suggests a more cautious picture, however: Mao (2026) argues that AI-generated dramas may sit alongside rather than replace live-action production, with AI better suited to effects-heavy genres and hybrid workflows, while live action remains stronger for emotionally complex storytelling. Current AI research similarly suggests that future short-drama production may depend less on isolated tools for script-writing, dubbing or visual effects, and more on integrated pipelines that coordinate narrative pacing, character continuity, spatial layout, scene transitions and music across multiple production stages (Shi et al. 2026).

## Emerging genres and narrative experiments

Romance remains the top-performing category, often in melodramatic forms such as CEO love stories or period fantasies (Daswani 2025b). Across 2024–2026, however, genre change in vertical micro-drama is better understood as cyclical rather than linear. Diversification has become increasingly visible in global-facing coverage and platform catalogues, but many of these genres and hybrids had already circulated within Chinese and English-language vertical drama ecosystems. Genres are rapidly adopted, intensified through repetition, saturated, and then abandoned or temporarily displaced before reappearing in revised or hybridised forms under platform-driven production conditions. Creators are therefore not simply moving beyond tried-and-tested tropes; they are recycling, recombining and reactivating them in response to audience demand, platform analytics and genre fatigue.

## Horror and thriller in vertical form

One area of recent platform experimentation is micro-horror series targeting mobile viewers. In November 2025, the niche streaming service Screamify, known for independent horror films, launched a dedicated 'Micro Horrors' vertical on its app (Blacktooth 2025). Each Micro Horror$^{™}$ is a serialised horror story delivered through very short vertical episodes, with accelerated pacing and intense cliff-hangers designed to maximise tension quickly. 'It's about delivering maximum terror in minimal time – perfect for the modern horror fan who lives on their phone,' a Screamify spokesperson explains (Blacktooth 2025). The inaugural series, directed by genre filmmaker Trent Duncan, suggests that horror can be adapted to compressed vertical storytelling: one micro-horror followed a couple trapped in a cursed beach house, distilling the haunted-house structure into short serial instalments. By integrating these mini-thrillers directly into its app, Screamify gave horror audiences a new way to engage and offered independent horror creators an 'entirely new canvas for storytelling' in vertical form (Blacktooth 2025).

Beyond Screamify, horror-themed micro-dramas have also appeared on general platforms. This suggests that horror in vertical micro-drama should not be understood only through Screamify's trade-facing launch announcement, but as part of a wider platform experiment in adapting suspense, jump scares, family threat and supernatural tension to short-form serial viewing. For example, ReelShort released what was promoted as its first horror micro-series, We're Your Parents, in 2025, suggesting that established vertical-drama platforms were beginning to test whether suspense, family threat and supernatural tension could extend the format beyond its dominant romance and melodrama base. These experiments suggest a broadening of tone: short-form content is not only for light-hearted romance or comedy skits, but can also deliver suspense, jump scares and darker storytelling in a vertically framed, mobile-friendly way.

## Musical micros and beyond

Another experiment in this period was the development of musical micro-dramas. In January 2026, HolyWater and Second Rodeo announced Playback, billed as the first musical produced specifically for vertical video and scheduled for release on HolyWater's

My Drama app (HolyWater 2026a). Subsequent industry coverage reported that the series debuted on My Drama in April 2026 and featured 17 original songs across a roughly 100-minute runtime, with its production also notable for being SAG-affiliated under the emerging verticals agreement (Patton 2026). While substantial critical reviews of the series have not yet emerged, trade coverage frames Playback as an attempt to test whether a genre associated with music, performance and choreography can be adapted to the compressed rhythms and production conditions of vertical micro-drama.

Similar experiments are also emerging around docu-drama, reality-style and lifestyle vertical series. In October 2025, Realscreen reported that Fox executives saw vertical series as an opportunity to experiment with unscripted formats, including reality, lifestyle and competition-based storytelling (Realscreen 2025). By June 2026, this had developed into a more concrete example: Fox Entertainment and HolyWater's My Drama launched a vertical adaptation of Farmer Wants a Wife Season 3, reworking an 11-episode primetime reality season into 101 short-form episodes for mobile viewing (HolyWater 2026b). HolyWater described the adaptation as the first time a Fox primetime reality series had been fully reimagined as a phone-native micro-drama, with episodes averaging under two minutes and organised around fast emotional hooks designed for mobile scrolling (HolyWater 2026b). This suggests that the micro-drama template is not being tested only through fictional romance, thriller or melodrama, but also through the reformatting of unscripted television, lifestyle formats and existing broadcast IP into mobile-first serial experiences.

A further example of genre diversification can be seen in culinary micro-drama. The Missing Master Chef, distributed on NetShort, adapts the hidden-identity and revenge structures common in vertical drama to a cooking-centred narrative. The platform synopsis describes a once-renowned chef who disappears after reaching the peak of his career, is betrayed and injured, loses the use of his hands, and later attempts to reclaim his status through a grassroots cooking competition (NetShort 2026). What makes the example significant is not simply its subject matter, but the way cooking provides a visually legible structure for short-form serial storytelling. Food preparation lends itself to close-ups, timed reveals, sensory reaction shots and procedural suspense: the cut, the stir, the smell, the tasting and the judgement can each become compressed dramatic beats. Real Reel's review makes this point explicitly, arguing that the series understands food as a form of visual storytelling in which 'technique is story' and each short episode combines a tease, a culinary action and a cliff-hanger (Real Reel 2025). In this sense, The Missing Master Chef suggests that vertical micro-drama can extend beyond romance, horror and fantasy by adapting procedural forms where skill, recognition and status reversal can be made immediately visible on a mobile screen.

## Blending genres – supernatural and niche themes

By 2023–2024, micro-dramas had already established a strong tendency towards genre mashup, particularly through combinations of romance with supernatural, sci-fi and thriller elements. This pattern continues into the 2024–2026 period through renewed cycles of iteration and platform amplification. Werewolf, vampire and other supernatural romance subgenres were already widely circulated and commercially successful across 2023 and 2024 platforms, where they functioned as recurring templates rather than late-stage.

innovations in 2025. Early werewolf vertical dramas frequently combined supernatural romance with CEO-driven power fantasy narratives, producing hybrid story structures in which corporate hierarchy and supernatural transformation operated as parallel forms of dominance and desire.

Many of the top-trending series on TikTok’s micro-drama app PineDrama also revolve around romance-fantasy hybrids. Love at First Bite, a vampire love story, rapidly reached 18 million views, while multiple PineDrama hits feature ‘sordid love affairs with vampires or werewolves,’ often mixing romance with paranormal intrigue (Moses and Whateley 2026). PineDrama’s top-three shows upon launch each exceeded 100 million views, with titles such as The Officer Fell For Me, a forbidden romance with a police officer, reaching 192 million views, and The Awakening of the Returned Heiress, a family saga with a ghostly twist, reaching 99 million views (Moses and Whateley 2026). These examples extend patterns already visible in China’s micro-drama boom, where fantasy-tinged romances and exaggerated soap-opera plots provided adaptable templates for global-facing production.

Beneath these shifting genre labels, however, a more persistent Cinderella structure often remains in place: protagonists move from marginalisation, humiliation or hidden identity towards recognition, romantic validation and social elevation. This underlying structure helps explain why CEO romances, werewolf dramas, supernatural fantasies and revenge melodramas can appear as different genres while still repeating a shared narrative logic of transformation, status reversal and emotional reward (Gist 2025, 193–194).

Western creators are also extending earlier experiments with Boys' Love (BL) and LGBTQ+ themes in vertical drama. Cover Story had already brought BL themes to vertical audiences in March 2024, reportedly achieving strong viewership, while Straight Until He Kissed Me is a 2025 micro-drama that puts an LGBTQ+ spin on the classic billionaire romance, as a straight male protagonist fakes an affair and unexpectedly falls for a male CEO. The audience for Cover Story also appears to have expanded after some platforms adopted free-to-air access models, suggesting that discoverability and platform payment structures can significantly shape the circulation of BL vertical content. Cover Story also developed characters who appeared in My Moonlit Alpha Prince, with both titles dated to March 2024. These examples indicate that BL and LGBTQ+ themes should not be framed as newly emerging in 2025, but as part of an earlier and continuing cycle of genre adaptation within English-language vertical drama.

Recent platform catalogues also indicate a widening of visible themes and audience segments. These include short-form series about life after 50; such as My Husband Turned into a Billionaire, workplace dramas such as My Boss Likes My Cosplay, which blends office romance with geek culture, and other creative premises within current global-facing catalogues (Moses and Whateley 2026). Genre conventions are therefore not only being respected and subverted, but repeatedly recycled across platform cycles, with mafia dramas, witch narratives and other supernatural or crime-inflected templates moving through phases of intense production, audience fatigue, apparent decline and later revival in new hybridised forms. This cyclical pattern reflects platform-driven commissioning strategies in which previously saturated or commercially underperforming genres are periodically reactivated when analytics and audience segmentation indicate renewed demand or cross-market potential.

Nevertheless, one editor notes that each micro-series still ‘adheres to specific, well-defined tropes’ of its genre in order to hook audiences quickly (Stojan 2025). The examples above show how those tropes can also be modified through cross-genre combination, representational reframing or niche audience targeting: Love at First Bite merges vampire romance with platform-native melodrama, Straight Until He Kissed Me reworks billionaire romance through BL themes, and My Boss Likes My Cosplay combines workplace romance with geek-culture performance (Moses and Whateley 2026; Stojan 2025).

<a id="s16"></a>
## Adapting existing IP

Adaptation has long been central to vertical drama, particularly through the well-established pathway from Chinese web novels and web fiction into serialised vertical screen formats. Gist’s account of writing Chinese-financed vertical series provides a practitioner example of this pathway: his collaborations involved adapting Chinese web novels, revising storylines supplied by app development teams and developing original material through negotiations over how Chinese-originated narrative structures might be made culturally legible to Western audiences (Gist 2025, 192–193). This adaptive logic also includes extensive replication, reiteration and remaking of successful story concepts, where proven narrative structures are repeatedly reworked across platforms, markets and production cycles. Final Call for Love, for example, has been cited as a successful story concept that has been repeatedly remade or reiterated, illustrating how vertical drama often develops through recycling and recombination rather than wholly original IP creation.

In the 2024–2026 period, this practice also became visible through the reformatting of existing television, romance and franchise IP into micro-drama instalments. One recent English-language example was The Secret Life of Amy Bensen, a romance thriller novel series that had been made into a conventional streaming show in 2022. In a partnership between Passionflix, the original producer, and Vigloo, The Secret Life of Amy Bensen was reimagined as a 35-episode vertical micro-series to 'introduce the story to an entirely new audience' (Daswani 2025b). The micro-drama version repackaged the plot into brisk episodes under two minutes each and was dubbed in multiple languages to capitalise on Vigloo's global user base (Daswani 2025b). Reporting on the Passionflix and Vigloo partnership, Daswani suggests that the vertical adaptation allowed existing fans to encounter the story in a new format, while also introducing the IP to new viewers through a more accessible short-form structure (Daswani 2025b).

This example points to the continuing importance of adaptation rather than a shift towards original IP as the dominant norm. Popular web novels, romance fiction, webtoons, television shows and other existing story assets can find a second life as vertical micro-dramas, extending their reach through shorter, lower-cost and more mobile-friendly formats. In this sense, micro-drama can function as a short-form extension of existing franchises, using familiar story worlds and proven narrative assets while adapting them to the rhythms of mobile-first serial viewing (Daswani 2025b).

<a id="s17"></a>
## Viewer interaction and evolving audience expectations

The expansion of vertical drama has been shaped not only by creators and platforms, but also by audiences who have rapidly embraced this way of consuming stories. Across the

2024–2026 period, viewer behaviours and expectations around micro-dramas became increasingly evident through platform data, fandom activity and industry reporting, revealing opportunities for deeper interaction and innovation in how stories are delivered.

<a id="s18"></a>
## Bingeable and habit-forming content

One clear pattern is that audiences treat micro-dramas as compact narrative instalments that encourage continued viewing. Successful creators and platforms have learned to build on this by structuring release schedules and engagement tactics accordingly. Many series now release new episodes daily, or even multiple times a day, at consistent times, creating a form of appointment viewing on mobile. Regular episode drops keep fans returning to the app, much as soap operas did in the television era. Deloitte analysts note that this steady cadence helps build loyal followings; over time, popular micro-dramas can become 'mini-soap franchises' with ongoing story arcs spanning seasons and even spin-offs (Arkenberg et al. 2025). For example, a hit such as Cohabitating Romance might run for more than 50 episodes, then generate a sequel series or a spin-off focused on side characters. By May 2026, this soap-like tendency had become more explicit in India through Story TV Dailies, a vertical format built around open-ended serials with no defined conclusion, daily episode drops and writing intended to evolve with audience preferences (ET Bureau 2026). The model shifts micro-drama from a finite package designed for rapid bingeing towards continuous mobile seriality.

Viewers therefore come to expect continuity, looking for short-form content that is not simply a sequence of one-off viral clips, but a story that progresses and pays off over time. In this sense, micro-dramas combine the instant gratification of social media with the longer-term engagement of television serials. This addresses a modern attention paradox: younger generations using infinite feeds often feel unsatisfied or 'overwhelmed' by disjointed content (Arkenberg et al. 2025). Short-form serials offer one response to this condition. As one industry report suggests, a 'throwback to linear TV' in miniature might provide more narrative sustenance in a fragmented attention economy (Scanlon 2025). Rather than offering only disconnected scrolling content, micro-dramas provide a brief emotional journey and a cliff-hanger to anticipate. This renewed appetite for serialised storytelling is a key audience shift enabling micro-dramas to thrive.

<a id="s19"></a>
## Multi-platform engagement and community

Creators are also finding that audiences want ways to engage beyond simply watching a 90-second episode. Some producers have therefore experimented with companion content and interactive community features around micro-dramas. For instance, UK-based Spirit Studios, when launching its first vertical drama project, revealed plans to produce companion podcasts, behind-the-scenes clips and other audience experiences alongside the short video episodes (Daswani 2025a). Such material can extend the story world and give fans additional touchpoints between episode releases, whether through character backstory, actor commentary or speculation around future plot developments.

Micro-drama apps and social platforms are also adapting to serialised viewing habits. In-app discussion threads, polls, comments and reaction videos create a sense of

participation, even when the story itself is not fully choice-based. The TikTok series Group Chat, discussed earlier, illustrates this dynamic: viewers did not only watch the unfolding text-message drama, but also role-played in comments and anticipated characters' next moves, turning the series into a shared communal experience. Recognising this shift, TikTok experimented with a 'Minis' section for series in its main app and, by early 2026, launched PineDrama as a separate app to curate and organise episodic shorts (Moses and Whateley 2026). One challenge with traditional social feeds is that they make it difficult to follow a series in order, so platforms are adapting features such as 'continue watching' rails and series playlists to facilitate narrative viewing (Arkenberg et al. 2025). These developments indicate an emerging expectation that platforms should support continuity, discovery and return viewing once audiences invest in a micro-drama.

<a id="s20"></a>
## Higher standards for quality and representation

As the audience for vertical dramas broadens, viewers and platforms are placing greater emphasis on polished and diverse content. Some micro-dramas now command large, cross-demographic fanbases, and viewership is not limited to teenagers. A Digiday survey in 2025 found that nearly half of global internet users watching mini-dramas were aged 18–34, while in some regions more 35–44-year-olds watched micro-dramas than 18–24-year-olds (Scanlon 2025). This suggests that millennials and older Gen Z viewers, many of whom have grown up with high-quality television and film, are significant consumers of the format and bring corresponding expectations.

This trend has been reinforced by continuing production investment and talent involvement. Fox's partnership with HolyWater was initially framed around Hollywood writers and recognised actors, including Jon Hamm and Minnie Driver, contributing to upcoming vertical series (Fischer and Flynn 2026). By 2026, that interest had developed into a broader production push, with Fox linked to large-scale vertical-series plans through HolyWater and a separate multiyear deal with Dhar Mann Studios for scripted vertical shows (Spangler 2025; 2026). Likewise, former Miramax executive Bill Block launched GammaTime in 2025 with $14M funding, explicitly aiming to apply professional filmmaking expertise to micro content (Manfredi 2025), and the platform attracted further investment in 2026 (Weprin 2026). These developments suggest that micro-drama is increasingly being treated as a professionalised production space rather than only as low-budget mobile content. Hoorae Media's Screen Time provided a recent example of this professionalisation: a 57-part TikTok thriller produced with a predominantly Black cast and union-scale working conditions, using one-minute episodes to combine mobile-first distribution with a more polished thriller aesthetic (Davies 2026).

At the same time, audiences are looking for more variety in stories and characters. There is growing demand for micro-dramas that reflect different cultures and perspectives, moving beyond the repetitive billionaire-romance template. This international circulation builds on the longer China-led development of the form: Chinese companies and platforms have been adapting stories for Western and English-language audiences for several years, while Chinese, Korean and Indian short dramas continue to be translated, adapted and promoted to global viewers (Arkenberg et al. 2025). Spanish-language micro-dramas are also expanding: Mexico's Elefantec Global struck a deal to produce

Spanish micro-series for HolyWater's app, anticipating demand among Hispanic audiences in the U.S. and Latin America (Mendoza 2025).

These developments indicate that micro-drama is being treated as a more legitimate entertainment format, one expected to meet higher standards of storytelling quality, performance and inclusivity. In response, SAG-AFTRA introduced a tailored Verticals Agreement to give micro-drama producers a route to hire union actors on lower-budget vertical projects while providing performers with clearer protections (SAG-AFTRA 2025; Tapp 2025). This kind of labour infrastructure further signals the format's movement into professional screen-industry practice, while also potentially improving consistency in performance quality for audiences.

<a id="s21"></a>
## Audience as drivers of innovation

Perhaps the most significant shift in audience behaviour is the extent to which viewers actively shape the evolution of the format. Micro-drama fans are highly vocal online, and their comments, viewing patterns and bingeing behaviour can provide immediate signals about what is working. Data-driven creative loops have long been central to platform-native micro-drama, with algorithms and technology companies shaping commissioning, story iteration and audience targeting for several years. Gist likewise observes that the speed of vertical commissioning and production, combined with rapid analysis of audience engagement, allows app-based producers to ‘course correct’ more quickly than conventional television production cycles generally permit (Gist 2025, 191). By 2025, these practices were becoming more visible in English-language industry accounts of production, with creators described as tweaking storylines on the fly in response to audience sentiment (Arkenberg et al. 2025). This longer history is important because platform analytics should be understood not as a recent innovation, but as part of the form’s established development through algorithmic recommendation, audience testing, rapid commissioning and technology-company involvement.

Cao et al. (2026) give a concrete example of this process: in one production, repeated audience comments praising a second male lead led the team to increase that character's scenes and close-ups, after which the authors report substantial increases in likes, comments and shares by episode four. They describe this as part of a wider mode of audience-responsive micro-drama, in which writers adapt storylines in response to real-time feedback, including comments, reposts and memes (Cao et al. 2026). This is not the same as explicit branching or choose-your-own-adventure storytelling. Rather, it is a more indirect form of audience participation in which writers infer audience preferences from platform data and social response, then incorporate those interpretations into later production decisions.

However, this feedback loop should not be treated as a transparent expression of collective audience desire. Cao et al. (2026) caution that comments and engagement data may over-represent highly active users, be shaped by traffic boosting or paid promotion, and may even include AI-generated comments that simulate community activity. Audience-responsive production therefore creates new opportunities for agility and participation, but also introduces problems of representativeness, authenticity and creative over-dependence on platform-visible signals. In this sense, the audience is not passive, but neither is audience participation fully democratic: sustaining engagement

increasingly depends on how creators and platforms interpret, filter and operationalise audience data.

<a id="s22"></a>
## Conclusion and outlook

During the 2024–2026 period, vertical micro-drama has become firmly established as a significant mobile-first storytelling form. Its recent international visibility should be understood as the acceleration and global circulation of practices developed through earlier Chinese and platform-native production contexts. This rapid evolution has been fuelled by creative experimentation at multiple levels: narrative techniques ranging from branching storylines to chat-based dramas, AI-assisted production tools, genre recombination and deeper forms of audience interaction.

Looking ahead, these trends indicate possible directions for further development, but they should be understood as emerging tendencies rather than settled outcomes. Developments emerging during the article's final revision in July 2026 – including post-episode character interaction, open-ended daily serials and AI-native synthetic casts – suggest that the next phase may alter not only genre and production methods, but the boundaries of the micro-drama episode itself (C21Media 2026; ET Bureau 2026; Pulliam-Moore 2026). Industry experts predict more entrants in 2026 as major media companies and technology platforms make their own vertical content plays. Commenting on TikTok's PineDrama launch, one media consultant notes: 'It's a sign that vertical video is evolving … you're going to see more announcements this year from many other companies.' With intensifying competition, the creative bar may continue to rise. Current industry activity suggests possible movement towards more interactive formats, greater personalisation through AI and higher production values, although the extent and durability of these developments remain uncertain. Recent AI research reinforces this uncertainty: systems such as One Sentence, One Drama suggest that AI-generated short drama remains dependent on solving problems of hook design, narrative coherence, spatial continuity and production-level review, rather than simply generating video from prompts (Shi et al. 2026). At the same time, the core appeal of the format – short, emotionally immediate storytelling optimised for mobile viewing – may remain its organising principle. As editor Ren Chih Chen advises fellow creators, 'Treat vertical storytelling as its own cinematic language, not as a limitation … use the format's strengths – intimacy, immediacy, and direct connection' (Stojan 2025). Those strengths align with contemporary audience appetites, provided creators continue to test what can be achieved in highly compressed serial episodes (Stojan 2025).

In summary, the creative developments visible across 2024–2026 suggest that vertical micro-drama is moving from platform-native serial melodrama into a more international, AI-assisted, genre-cyclical and industrially contested screen form. Vertical micro-dramas have shown that they can be popular, commercially effective and, in some cases, formally inventive. For content creators, this represents a new canvas with distinctive rules: a space where agility, experimentation and audience collaboration can be rewarded, but where speed, repetition and data-driven risk management may also limit innovation. This tension is also evident in Cao et al.'s (2026) account of feedback-driven production, where the pressure to satisfy early engagement metrics can lead to formulaic storytelling, creator burnout and audience fatigue. The micro-drama boom is reshaping the rules of

visual storytelling for the mobile age, while also raising questions about how much creative change a rapidly scaling platform industry can sustain.
